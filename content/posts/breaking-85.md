Three days later and I'm still replaying it.

Not the good shots — though there were good shots — but the decision on hole 12 that could have unraveled everything. A 185-yard par three, front pin, the flag barely moving. I had a 6-iron in my hand. My playing partner said, "That's at least a 5." I hit the 6.

Landed eight feet from the hole. Made birdie. Finished with an 83.

## How It Happened

I'd been trending in the right direction for six weeks. My handicap had crept from 18.3 down to 16.1 — nothing dramatic, but steady. The range sessions were paying off. More importantly, I'd made a deal with myself: no hero shots. Take your medicine. Three-putt avoidance above all else.

The round itself was unremarkable through the first six holes. Two pars, four bogeys. Standard fare. Then something clicked on seven.

I made a thirty-foot putt for par on seven. Then a ten-footer for bogey save on eight that felt like a win. And suddenly I was *in* it — that rare focused state where each shot only contains itself, nothing before or after.

> The secret of golf is to turn three shots into two.

I've read that quote a hundred times. I finally understood it somewhere around hole nine.

## The Numbers

- **Score:** 83 (38 out, 45 in — wait, actually reversed: 45 out, 38 in)
- **Fairways hit:** 9 of 14
- **Greens in regulation:** 8 of 18
- **Putts:** 28
- **Birdies:** 2
- **Bogeys:** 10
- **Doubles:** 1 (the third hole, never speak of it again)

Twenty-eight putts. That's the number. When you're putting that well, golf becomes a different game.

## What Breaking 85 Actually Means

For three years my barrier was 90. Then I broke 90 and my barrier became 85. These numbers are arbitrary — a golfer shooting 84 is not objectively twice the player as someone shooting 94 — but they serve as psychological checkpoints.

I'm now within striking distance of breaking 80. That's the real target. The 70s. I've played with people who live in the 70s and they don't seem to be doing anything dramatically different. They just *make decisions differently*. They accept their miss patterns. They know when to lay up and they do it without grief.

That's the work ahead.
