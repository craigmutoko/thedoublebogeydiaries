I'd been dreaming about Pebble Beach since I first picked up a club.

Not because I saw it on television, though I did — but because my father had a framed print of the seventh hole on the wall of his study for my entire childhood. That tiny par three with the Pacific stretching out behind it, the green perched on the cliff like something too beautiful to be real. He never got to play it.

I played it on a Wednesday afternoon in late spring, with rain threatening from the north and the wind stiff off Stillwater Cove. I made bogey. It was perfect.

## The Experience

You can read a thousand descriptions of Pebble Beach and none of them quite prepare you. The photos can't carry the *scale* of it — how the cliffs drop away into cold water, how the ocean sounds like applause and threat simultaneously, how small you feel on the fifth tee looking down toward Carmel Bay.

My caddie's name was Joel. He'd been looping at Pebble for fourteen years. He knew the breaks on every green and, more usefully, he knew the smart miss on every hole. "Don't be left on eight," he told me on the eighth tee. "Left is dead. Right is trouble but it's recoverable trouble." I went right on eight. I recovered.

## The Holes That Got Me

**Hole 7** — The famous short par three. 102 yards from the whites, playing into 20 mph of crosswind. I hit a pitching wedge and it came out okay. Two-putted for bogey. Did not feel like a bogey.

**Hole 8** — The most dramatic hole I've ever stood on. The drive is blind over a cliff edge. You cannot see the fairway. Joel said "175 to the left edge, aim at the ocean" and I obeyed. The ball disappeared. When we walked to the top of the hill and I saw it in the short grass, I laughed out loud.

**Hole 18** — I bogeyed it. The scorecard says 91. I will tell people I shot 91 at Pebble Beach for the rest of my life.

## On Playing Sacred Courses

There's a pressure to these rounds that can ruin them. I felt it on the first tee — fifty people watching, this famous opening hole, my hands shaking slightly. I pushed my drive right and made double.

Then I let it go. This course has seen Tom Watson chip in, Tiger at 12-under, Jack winning in 1972. It has seen every manner of human performance. It doesn't need me to be great. It just needs me to be present.

That turned out to be the best golf advice I've ever given myself.
